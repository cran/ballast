Designing for a robust result

A trial is usually sized so that a true effect of a given size is significant with probability 0.8 or 0.9. The stress test adds a second question: with what probability will that significant result also be robust, so that no pre-specified alternative analysis or single deletion moves it across the significance level, outside the interval or across zero? Simulation shows the two probabilities differ. At conventional power 0.8 roughly a third of significant results are classed sensitive, because a p-value that lands near alpha is what a correctly sized trial produces much of the time. At power 0.95 the share falls to about one in six.

stress_design() makes this a design quantity. It simulates the planned trial over a grid of sample sizes and effect sizes, runs the light stress test on each simulated trial and reports the robustness power, the probability of a significant and robust result, next to the conventional power.

library(ballast)
des <- stress_design(n_per_arm = c(50, 75, 100, 150), delta = c(0, 4.5),
                     n_visits = 4, sigma = 10, rho = 0.6, dropout = 0.03,
                     covariance = "ar1h", method = "Satterthwaite", vcov = "Asymptotic",
                     n_trials = 200, cores = 8)
des
plot(des)

The print method names the smallest sample size per arm that reaches a target robustness power (0.8 by default) for each non-zero effect. The plot draws both probabilities against the sample size, one panel per effect size, with the null panel showing the type I error and the share of false positives that would be flagged.

What the cells report

For every (sample size, effect) cell the table gives:

  • power and robustness_power: significant, and significant and robust.
  • robust_given_sig, sensitive_given_sig, fragile_given_sig: the verdict rates among significant results. Under the null these describe what a false positive looks like to the stress test.
  • marginal_given_sig: how often the marginal flag would be set.
  • k1_given_sig: how often removing one subject loses significance. With fragility = TRUE the greedy deletion search runs instead and k_reached_given_sig and k_median describe the fragility index.
  • tipping_margin and tipping_absorbed, with tipping = TRUE: the shift applied to imputed active-arm values at which p crosses alpha, as a multiple of the effect, and the share of significant trials that absorb the whole grid.
  • max_shift_se: the median, over trials, of the largest shift in pre-specified standard errors under the families.

A small illustration

Three trials per cell is far too few for a real design; 200 per cell with the deletion family is a run of minutes to an hour depending on the sample size, since the deletion family refits once per subject.

Writing it into the analysis plan

The expected rates belong in the statistical analysis plan next to the pre-specified stress test. stress_sap() writes the plan text; a sentence such as “under the design assumptions the probability of a significant and robust result is 0.72 and about one significant result in four is expected to be classed sensitive” gives the verdict after unblinding a pre-registered comparison. A sensitive verdict then reads as the expected consequence of a p-value near alpha rather than as a discovery, and a fragile verdict for a significant result, which the simulations put at well under one in a hundred, reads as the warning it is.

Assumptions in the simulator

simulate_trial() draws AR(1) errors with a common residual SD, a treatment effect growing linearly to delta at the last visit, a baseline covariate, and dropout after each visit that depends on the current value. Heavy tails, heteroscedastic visits, dropout depending on the unobserved next value and effect modification by baseline are available as settings. The pre-specified covariance in the analysis need not be the true one; the covariance family always includes the structures named in alternatives.
